Volume on the recess conversation amplifies as parents and policymakers push for more play

New pediatric guidance, a parent-led movement, and landmark laws in Tennessee and Oklahoma are putting unstructured play back on the agenda. End Chronic Disease is making the case that recess is chronic disease prevention.

Just a few months after the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) issued its first updated guidance on recess in 13 years, the national conversation around protecting play during the school day continues to gain momentum.

A new Wall Street Journal report highlights a growing parent-led movement to restore recess in schools, as families across the country raise concerns about shrinking playtime, student burnout, and children’s social development. Parents in states including Tennessee, Michigan, New Hampshire, New York, and beyond are organizing locally and pushing lawmakers to guarantee more unstructured play during the school day.

The movement has already led to a couple of landmark state policy wins. Tennessee, for example, enacted a law requiring at least 40 minutes of daily recess for elementary students, while Oklahoma doubled its requirement from 20 to 40 minutes. These changes make Tennessee and Oklahoma two of the strongest states in the country when it comes to recess policy. Parents and lawmakers in several other states are pursuing similar changes, and End Chronic Disease is staying closely involved.

The renewed attention comes on the heels of the AAP’s updated policy statement, which reflects more than a decade of new research linking recess to children’s learning, memory, executive function, social development, mental health, and physical well-being.

Responding to the Journal’s reporting, End Chronic Disease CEO Kelly McKenna authored a letter to the editor, “Never Doubt the Importance of a Jungle Gym,” highlighting recess as a simple, low-cost tool for chronic disease prevention. With up to 80% of children falling short of recommended daily physical activity levels, McKenna argues that protecting time to move during the school day can help children build healthier habits and reduce their long-term risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and other chronic diseases. We’re proud to share that the Journal published this letter on July 27 both online and in the print edition.

Additionally, McKenna joined Fox News anchor Martha MacCallum on a brand-new episode of The Untold Story with Martha MacCallum, Martha’s podcast run through FOX News Radio, and the two co-authored an op-ed titled “America’s next 250 years begin on the playground,” published by FOX News. The piece made the case that recess isn’t time taken away from learning. It is an important part of how children learn, connect, develop resilience, and build the skills they will carry into adulthood. As we celebrate 250 years of America and look ahead to the next 250, recess is vital for social development, learning, behavior, and protecting children against long-term chronic disease risk, beginning at a young age.

With parents organizing, pediatricians weighing in, lawmakers considering new requirements, and national media increasingly focused on the issue, a simple idea is gaining the attention it deserves: children need time during the school day to move, play, and be kids. As the AAP’s updated policy statement notes, recess also provides children with opportunities to relieve stress, strengthen emotional well-being, and build the social and coping skills that help them navigate adversity and trauma. Combined with its benefits for physical activity, these experiences reinforce why protecting recess is an important part of promoting lifelong health and preventing chronic disease.
